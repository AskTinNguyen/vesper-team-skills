# Write Clearly — ORDER OF WORDS IN A SENTENCE (Rules 15–45)

Part II: how to arrange a sentence so it is clear (unambiguous) and forcible. The unified
ideas: **emphatic words go in emphatic positions; related words stay close; hold the reader
in suspense; repeat what omission would muddy; and build upward (climax).**

## Emphatic positions — 15, 15a, 15b, 16, 17, 18
- **15:** Emphatic words belong at the **beginning or end** of a sentence. "He quickly left the
  room" (neutral); for emphasis, "he left **quickly**" at the end. An adverb's default home is
  between subject and verb; move it only to emphasize it.
- **15a:** Keep **unemphatic** words from the end. "To know some Latin… is useful" → "It is
  **useful** to know some Latin." Add an emphatic adverb to justify an end-position
  ("is very useful"). Avoid a short "**chippy**" ending ("the soldier… writhed" → "fell
  writhing to the ground"). Exceptions: prepositions/pronouns attached to emphatic words
  stay at the end. Don't make final emphasis monotonous.
- **15b:** An **interrogation** gives emphasis: "No one can doubt…" → "**Who can doubt**…?"
- **16:** An unusually emphatic **subject** is moved from the beginning: "**Thomas** built this
  house" neutral; "This house was built **by Thomas**," or "**It was Thomas that** built this
  house." (A subject repeated as the first word across consecutive sentences rises in
  emphasis on its own and need not move.)
- **17:** The **object** may precede the verb for emphasis (common in antithesis): "**Some he
  imprisoned, others he put to death.**" Use sparingly in prose — it can create ambiguity
  ("the son the father slew").
- **18:** Where several words are emphatic, make clear **which is the most emphatic** — by
  subordinating the rest, adding an intensifier ("every conceivable device"), or antithesis
  ("not only your skill but also your fidelity").

## Proximity — 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26
- **19:** Words must be as near as possible to the words they are grammatically connected with.
- **20:** Adverbs next to the word they qualify. "I told him to go slowly, but he left the room
  quickly, dropping the purse" — *quickly* modifies *left* or *dropping*? Unemphatic adverbs
  sit between subject and verb; emphatic ones after the verb; fix ambiguity by reordering or
  using coordinate verbs.
- **21:** **"Only"** strict rule: place it **before the word it affects.** "He only beat three"
  (did nothing but beat) || "He beat only three" (no more than three) || "He beat three only"
  (that was all he did — Abbott's archaic/rare sense; a modern reader reads this as "no more
  than three," so don't "fix" toward it). Avoid placing "only" between two emphatic words;
  prefer "alone" where it fits.
- **22:** After **"not only…but also,"** each must be followed by the **same part of speech.**
  "He not only gave me advice but also help" (wrong: verb then noun) → "He gave me **not only
  advice but also help.**" "He **not only gave** me a grammar, **but also lent** me a dictionary"
  (right).
- **23:** **"At least," "always,"** &c. can be ambiguous when between two emphatic words.
  "My Latin exercise, at all events, as good as my cousin's" — reorder to tie it to the right
  term: for "though not my other exercises" → "My Latin exercise, at all events, you will
  find…"; for "though not very good" → "…as good as my cousin's, at all events."
- **24:** Nouns near the nouns they **define**: "The death is announced of Mr John Smith."
- **25:** Pronouns follow the nouns they refer to **without another noun intervening.**
  Avoid "John Smith, the son of Thomas Smith, **who** gave me this book" unless Thomas is the
  antecedent. The more emphatic of two nouns may win, but the safer rule is **proximity** —
  don't leave yourself at the mercy of commas.
- **26:** Clauses that are grammatically connected stay **close together**; avoid parentheses
  that break the link (but see Brevity 55 for the short parenthetical).

## Suspense — 30, 30a, 31, 32, 33, 34
- **30:** Write so the reader feels the sentence **incomplete until the full stop** — keep them
  in suspense by putting if-clauses first, participles before what they qualify, and using
  suspensive conjunctions. Contrast a "dragging" sentence that could end at any of eight
  points with its suspended rewrite.
- **30a:** Don't end a long sentence with a **short unemphatic clause** beginning in "not…"
  or "which…": "…industry, self-dependence, and frugality, and **not, as some say,
  wastefulness**" → "not, as some say, wastefulness, **but** industry, self-dependence, and
  frugality." Exception: an *intentionally* unexpected unemphatic sting (epigram).
- **31:** **Suspense must not be excessive** — the "all subject, no verb yet" sentence. Break over-
  long suspense into coordinate English: "When X had happened, he did Y. Soon afterwards…"
- **32:** In an **"if/when/though"** sentence, put the **if-clause (protasis) first.** "Revenge thy
  father's most unnatural murder, if thou didst ever love him" is flat; the suspended "If thou
  didst ever thy dear father love—" creates the force. When the if-clause comes last it must be very emphatic
  ("if you were only here").
- **33:** Gain suspense by placing a **participle or adjective that qualifies the subject before
  the subject**: "**Deserted by his friends,** he was forced to have recourse to…" Don't overdo
  the French-style cascading participle; convert to a verb when it becomes a pile-up.
- **34:** **Suspensive conjunctions** (either…or, not only…but, on the one hand…, partly…partly,
  in the first place, it is true…doubtless) signal the shape of the sentence from the start.
  "**Either** you must take this perilous course… **or else** the liberty of your country is
  endangered."

## Repetition — 27, 28, 29, 35, 36, 37, 37a, 38, 40a
- **27:** In conditionals keep **antecedent (if) clauses distinct from consequent clauses.**
- **28:** Keep **dependent "that" clauses distinct from independent** ones; repeat "that" or
  insert a conjunction/full stop when the subject would otherwise be ambiguous.
- **29:** Keep **infinitives dependent on the same word distinct** from others — "he wished to
  take his friend with him to visit the capital and to study medicine" (who studies?). Use
  "that…might," or "in order to," to disambiguate purpose.
- **35:** **Repeat the subject** when its omission would cause ambiguity — especially after a
  relative used as subject.
- **36:** **Repeat a preposition** after an intervening conjunction, especially when a verb and
  object intervene: "that helped all his companions… and (**to**) John Smith in particular."
- **37:** **Repeat conjunctions, auxiliary verbs, and pronominal adjectives** that several verbs
  depend on: "When we look back…—and, above all, (**when**) we refer…" Repeat "that" in
  reported speech to separate the writer's words from others'.
## 37a. Repeat Verbs after "than"/"as":
  "I think he likes me better than you" (than you
  like me, or than he likes you?) → "than you do." "Cardinal Richelieu hated Buckingham as
  sincerely as **did** the Spaniard Olivares."
- **38:** In a long sentence, **repeat the subject or a summary** of what was said: "Gold and
  cotton, banks and railways…—**these** are not the elements that constitute a great nation."
- **40a:** Do not introduce a **new construction** unexpectedly (parallelism): write
  "virtuous and accomplished," not "of many virtues and accomplished"; "riding or walking,"
  not "on foot or riding."

## Climax, bathos, epigram — 39, 40, 41, 42
- **39:** Build **climax** — beginning prepares middle, middle prepares end, in an ascent.
  "To gossip is a fault; to libel, a crime; to slander, a sin."
- **40:** A sudden descent is **bathos** — feebleness. Intentional bathos can be forcible
  ("Go, teach Eternal Wisdom how to rule— Then drop into thyself, and be a fool").
- **41:** **Antithesis** adds force and often clearness — it can even fix the meaning of an
  ambiguous word ("power gentle / obedience liberal"). Avoid excessive antithesis.
- **42:** **Epigram** — a short sentence expressing truth under an amusing incongruity
  ("Verbosity is cured by a large vocabulary"). A simple sharp antithesis.

## Sentence structure — 43, 44, 45
- **43:** Let each sentence have **one, and only one, principal subject of thought.** Avoid
  **heterogeneous** sentences that carry several subjects on one level; split them.
- **44:** Keep the **connection between sentences** with opening connecting words (adverbs used
  as conjunctions): *accordingly, therefore, however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in the
  first place, to sum up, in fact.* Avoid stringing sentences with bare "and"/"so" (Burnet's
  fault).
- **45:** A **short intervening sentence showing the transition** may be required between two
  long sentences when a bare conjunction is insufficient.

## Applying this skill
Rewrite a muddy draft rule-by-rule (15→45), or run the sentence-level checklist: one emphatic
idea at the front/back, related words adjacent, subject/verb close, if-clauses first,
suspense held but not over-held, no ambiguous pronoun, each sentence one subject, climax not
bathos, and every sentence visibly connected to its neighbor.
