# Save the Cat! Beat Sheet

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (feature-length 110-page frame). Page targets are approximate; mark the story beats, not exact pages.

## The 15 Beats

1. Opening Image (1): The "before" snapshot of the protagonist and the world; the tone, mood, and starting point. Must bookend with the Final Image.
2. Theme Stated (5): Someone (often not the hero) states the theme or argument the story will test.
3. Set-Up (1-10): Introduce the protagonist, their ordinary world, the cast, and the rules of the story. Plant what will be called back.
4. Catalyst (12): The inciting moment that knocks the ordinary world down; the first moment events turn.
5. Debate (12-25): The hero questions whether to act; the section must pose a question.
6. Break into Two (25): The hero commits and crosses into a new world; the adventure begins.
7. B Story (30): A new character or relationship thread starts, usually the "heart" or love/subplot that gives the hero a breather and a second stake.
8. Fun and Games (30-55): The promise of the premise; the audience seeing why they came. Bonds are formed, hurdles are fun, stakes are not yet raised.
9. Midpoint (55): A big turning point that raises the stakes; a false or real peak where "fun" usually ends.
10. Bad Guys Close In (55-75): Pressure mounts; the hero's gains and inner security are undermined.
11. All Is Lost (75): A whiff of death; the hero loses something real (symbolic death).
12. Dark Night of the Soul (75-85): The hero wallows and must dig deep.
13. Break into Three (85): The hero finds the answer/solution and re-commits.
14. Finale (85-110): The plan executes, new skills are tested, and the theme is proven.
15. Final Image (110): The "after" snapshot that mirrors the Opening Image and shows the change.

## Ten Genre Families (Promise of the Premise)

1. Monster in the House: sin plus survival (e.g., *Jaws*, *Alien*).
2. Golden Fleece: road trip whose real goal is the hero's internal growth (e.g., *Star Wars*).
3. Out of the Bottle: wish-fulfillment or comeuppance granting a power (e.g., *Liar Liar*, *Bruce Almighty*).
4. Dude with a Problem: an ordinary person caught in an extraordinary, unfair situation.
5. Rites of Passage: change-of-life stories driven by pain and transition.
6. Buddy Love: a love story that transforms the protagonist through another character.
7. Whydunit: the interesting question is motive, not just who; an emotional investigation.
8. Fool Triumphant: an undervalued underdog wins against a system.
9. Institutionalized: an individual vs. a group; the group's needs overwhelm the few.
10. Superhero: a "different" person in a mediocre world; the creation myth must win sympathy.

## Practical Checks

- The Catalyst should not drift late; tighten the set-up so the story turns early and economically.
- The Debates must ask a question the act break answers.
- The Fun and Games must deliver the genre's promise; if it doesn't, the beat sheet is mislabeled.
- The B Story must be present and must interact with the A story by the midpoint.
- Opening/Final Image bookends are mandatory; verify the "before/after" in both.
