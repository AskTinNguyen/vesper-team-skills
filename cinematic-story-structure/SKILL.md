# Cinematic Story Structure

## Overview

Turn a raw premise into a structurally sound screenplay or narrative using four complementary professional systems: Snyder's Hollywood beats, McKee's story design, the sequence method of *Screenwriting Down to the Atoms*, and Campbell's mythic journey. These systems reinforce each other; do not treat them as competing templates.

## Workflow

**1. Extract the premise.**
Write the premise as one compound sentence: protagonist plus desire plus obstacle plus stakes. Before outlining, resolve the protagonist's tangible goal and what is lost if they fail.

**2. Classify the genre.**
Read `references/save-the-cat-beats.md` and name the Save the Cat genre (Monster in the House, Golden Fleece, Out of the Bottle, Dude with a Problem, Rites of Passage, Buddy Love, Whodunit, Fool Triumphant, Institutionalized, Superhero). Each genre carries its own promise; keep the promise visible in every act.

**3. Place the 15 beats.**
Map the 15 Save the Cat beats onto page targets in a feature-length frame. Assign every beat a concrete dramatic action, not just a label. The Opening Image and Final Image must bookend (mirror) each other.

**4. Strengthen with story design.**
Apply the McKee principles in `references/mckee-principles.md`: choose the controlling idea/theme, engineer an inciting incident that upsets the balance of the character's life, and escalate through progressive complications toward crisis, climax, and resolution. Confirm every major turning point is driven by a significant, value-changing decision, not chance.

**5. Build sequences.**
Use the sequence method in `references/atoms-sequence-method.md` to group the beats into 8-14 story sequences ("legs" of the journey), then group those into three acts: 2-3 setup sequences, 5-7 complication sequences, 3-4 resolution sequences. Each sequence must have its own goal and end on an escalation or reversal.

**6. Construct scenes that turn.**
Check each scene for one of the four turn types from the sequence method: a change in strategy, a shift in power, the revelation of new information, or a situation-changing action. A scene that does not change the value at stake is either redundant or a deliberate beat that must be justified.

**7. Map the mythic arc when appropriate.**
For hero-driven genre or franchise work, overlay the stages in `references/mythic-journey.md` (Departure, Initiation, Return) to test whether the protagonist is being pushed through a complete transformation. Use it as a diagnostic, not as a rigid checklist.

**8. Write and self-check.**
Produce logline, beat sheet, sequence outline, then scene-by-scene outline. Verify: every act break shifts stakes, the midpoint raises them, the dark night leads to an earned final plan, and the protagonist ends changed in a way that was seeded in the set-up.
