# Coverage Workflow (Rosman: Shooting the Scene)

Source: Mark Rosman, *Shooting the Scene*. Coverage = the camera choices the director provides so the editor can construct the scene. It is organized into three parts: fundamentals, style/challenges, advanced.

## The Six Elements That Determine Coverage

1. The Script/Text: story, character, theme, genre, tone.
2. The Location and Physical Space.
3. Blocking: how the actors move through the space.
4. The Shot: size, lens, height, and movement.
5. Composition: how the frame is arranged.
6. The Edit: what cutting options your shots create.

## The Six Goals of Coverage

- Serve the story and character.
- Maintain continuity (screen direction, eyelines, spatial logic).
- Give the editor choices while keeping the scene "warm" (cuttable).
- Build toward the scene's climax.
- Express the director's style and the genre.
- Stay economical for the schedule and budget.

## Scene Analysis Before Planning

- Who is the main character we are following? What do they want?
- Why must this character act now? What is at stake?
- How does the character view the other characters in the scene?
- Where is the scene paced? Where is its climax?
- How does the scene express theme, genre, and tone?
- How are visuals and sound carrying that meaning?

## Blocking First

- Blocking comes before shot planning; the locations and the blocking shape the shots.
- Figure out blocking from the script first, then adapt it with actors and the real location.
- Move actors for a reason: to reveal, to escalate, to change power, to change geography, to serve the scene's turning point.
- Respect the 180-degree line: keep the camera on one side of the axis so spatial relationships stay consistent.

## Building the Coverage Package

For a basic two-person scene:

1. Master: establishes the space, positions, and geography.
2. Two-shot: keeps both actors in frame for the emotional unit.
3. Over-the-shoulder (OTS) shots: draw focus to one speaker while keeping the other.
4. Singles/close-ups: isolate line delivery and reactions.
5. Inserts and cutaways: cover actions, objects, and text for editing flexibility.

Plan coverage around the scene's turning points so the "hot" beats have the strongest isolation and the calm beats can breathe in wider frames.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Cutting only in the camera (no editorial options).
- Crossing the line without a setup that re-establishes geography.
- Planning more shots than the schedule allows.
- Shooting multiple masters when one plus good coverage suffices.
- Sticking to a plan that is failing on set.

## Advanced Coverage

- Three players: avoid the "deadly triangle"; anchor with eyelines and insert a pivot to keep sight lines clear.
- Walk-and-talks: plan the master lateral move, then isolate singles with consistent screen direction.
- Table scenes: use a round-table anchor and cut between paired eyelines rather than guessing.
- Cars: coordinate driver/passenger eyelines and car movement so the axis never flips.
- Multiple cameras: set the second camera to capture what the first cannot (reaction, different size, different side) without duplicating.

## The One-Shooting-Day Method

Rosman's Part 3 opens with a director's full shooting day. On set, re-read the shot list, re-block with the actors, adjust the plan to reality, and keep the crew informed so shots fall in a cuttable order.
