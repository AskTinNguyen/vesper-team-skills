# Write Clearly — WORDS (Rules 1–14b)

The book's Part I. Each rule is stated exactly as Abbott wrote it, with his example,
and an operational "check" you can run on your own text. Full original text with all
exercises is in the umbrella skill's reference file.

## 1. Use words in their proper sense
Write *"his apparent guilt"* → should be **evident** guilt. Abbott's confused pairs:
*conscious/aware, unnatural/supernatural, transpire/occur, circumstance/event,
reverse/converse, eliminate/elicit.* Also forbids using the *same* word in two senses in
one passage ("It is in my power… since I have power to do this…" — second "power" should
be "authority"). Also forbids the slovenly *nice, awfully, delicious, glorious*.

**Check:** For every abstract or Latinate word, ask (a) is it the exact sense? (b) is that
word/the word's root reused elsewhere in a different sense nearby?

## 2. Avoid exaggerations
*"inexhaustible supplies… would have almost sufficed for twice the population"* —
**inexhaustible** contradicts what follows. Loose words to watch: *unprecedented,
incalculable, very, stupendous,* and any superlative that the rest of the sentence doesn't
support.

**Check:** Delete every exaggeration and see if the sentence still holds. If a claim can't
survive the rest of the sentence, soften it.

## 3. Avoid useless circumlocution and "fine writing"
*"Her Majesty here partook of lunch"* → write **"lunched."** Don't use *apex* for *top, species*
for *kind, individual* for *man, assist* for *help.*

**Check:** Replace each multi-word euphemism with the plain one-word verb/noun. (See also
Brevity 47a — a phrase may be expressed by a word.)

## 4. Be careful with "not…and," "any," "only," "not…or," "that"
- **any** — "I am not bound to receive any messenger" = every, or a single? Use **every**
  or **a single.**
- **not** — "I do not intend to help you because you are my enemy" is ambiguous (is the
  reason attached to *intend* or *help*?). When "not" governs only part, split it out:
  "I intend to help you, not because you are my enemy…"
- **only** — "only" is ambiguous with "alone": "you only advise me to wait" = *you alone*
  (or *you only advise, instead of helping*) — see Rule 21; at the start of a sentence
  "only" often = *but*.
- **or after a negative** — "I do not want butter or honey" usually means *neither…nor*,
  but can mean "not both." When in doubt use **nor.**
- **that** — repeated "that" with different dependencies confuses: "I am so much surprised
  by this statement that I am desirous of resigning, that I scarcely know what to reply" →
  rewrite so each "that" clearly depends on its word.

**Check:** Flag every *not/and/or/only/any/that* and confirm the reader cannot attach it to
the wrong neighbor.

## 4a. Be careful with ambiguous words (e.g. "certain")
*"he has a certain property"* — *certain* meaning *some* vs *unfailing.* Also avoid the same
word in two senses side by side (*"He turned to the left and left the room"*), and words
that mislead ("to lie" mistaken for "to deceive").

## 5. Be careful with "he," "it," "they," "these," &c.
"He told his friend that if he did not feel better…" — which person? And avoid vague
*in this way / of this sort* summaries; repeat the noun. Note the two "it"s: **retrospective**
(pointing back: "Avoid indiscriminate charity: it is a crime") and **prospective** (pointing
forward: "It is a crime to give indiscriminately"). A prospective "it" can be removed by
making the infinitive the subject — "**To give indiscriminately is a crime.**"

**Check:** Highlight every pronoun and verify one, unambiguous antecedent within reach.

## 6 / 6a / 6b. Reported speech
- **6:** When third-person speech is ambiguous, report it in the **first person**: *"He told his
  friend that if he did not feel better…"* → "He said to his friend, 'If I (or you) don't feel
  better…'"
- **6a:** Use **third person** where exact words are unknown, unimportant, lengthy, or
  tedious.
- **6b:** "that" may be omitted in third-person speech: "He said that he took it ill…" →
  "'He took it ill,' he said, 'that…'"

## 7. Make clear what a Participle implies (when/while/though/that)
*"Republics… will not be desired at all, unaccompanied by courtly graces"* — does
*unaccompanied* mean *since they are* or *if they are*? *"Men walking (that walk / when they
walk) on ice sometimes fall"* — prefer *walking* = *when they walk*; use *that* for the
relative. A participle before the subject usually implies cause ("Seeing this, he retired");
otherwise use its plain participial meaning. When ambiguity threatens, write "on seeing,"
"while keeping," etc.

## 8. Use "who"/"which" for "and he/and it, for he/for it"; else "that"
**"Who" and "which" introduce a new fact about the antecedent; "that" is restrictive —
it completes or defines it.** "I heard this from the inspector, who (and he) heard it from
the guard that travelled with the train." (*inspector* complete, *guard* needs the clause.)
"Fetch me (all) the books that lie on the table, and also the pamphlets, which (and these)
you will find on the floor."
Exceptions (modern usage keeps who/which): (a) antecedent defined by a possessive;
(b) "that" separated and isolated from its verb; (c) antecedent already qualified by "that"
(avoid the ugly repeat); (d) after a preposition (you can't say "that I jumped beyond");
(e) after *those/others/several* used as pronouns; (f) after "that" as conjunction.

## 9. Do not use redundant "and" before "which"
*"I gave him a very interesting book for a present, and which cost me five shillings."*
In long sentences the error hides; fix by omitting "and" or repeating the noun before
"which."

## 10 & 10a'. Equivalents for the Relative + repeat the Antecedent
Replace a relative clause with:
(a) **participle** — "men thirsting for revenge" (but this can imply when/though — see 7);
(b) **infinitive** — "the first that entered → to enter"; "a man who will act dishonestly →
to act dishonestly";
(c) **whereby/wherein** for "by/in which" (antiquated);
(d) **if** — "The man that does not care for music is to be pitied → If a man does not care
for music, he is to be pitied" (useful in long sentences);
(e) **and this** — "which was all that could be expected → and this was all that…";
(f) **what** — "that which you ought to know → what you ought to know";
(g) **omission** of object-relative: "the man (that) you speak of."
**10a':** Repeat the antecedent where non-repetition causes ambiguity — especially after a
negative: "he would not even hear me, which I confess I had expected" (expected *he would* or
*wouldn't*?) → "a refusal (or a favour) that I confess I had expected."

## 11. Use particular for general terms
*"I have neither the necessaries of life nor the means of procuring them"* → "I have not a
**crust of bread, nor a penny to buy one.**" Caution: a particular may exaggerate or be
imperfect. In science/philosophy, where inclusiveness matters, keep general terms.

## 11a. Avoid Verbal Nouns where Verbs can be used
*"notwithstanding his writing on the Revelation, and affecting to enter on all occasions into
controversy"* → "notwithstanding **that he wrote and affected**…" Verbal nouns get confused
with participles.

## 12. Use a particular Person instead of a class
"What is the splendour of the greatest monarch…" → "the splendour of **Solomon** compared
with the beauty of a **daisy.**" Also the forcible noun-for-adjective: "This fortress is
**weakness itself.**" Don't overdo it into bombast.

## 13. Use metaphor instead of literal statement
"The ship **ploughs** the sea" is clearer than "cleaves" and shorter than "cleaves as a
plough cleaves the land."

## 14 / 14a / 14b. Manage metaphor
- **14: Do not confuse metaphor.** "a thunderbolt… **deluging** their country with invaders"
(mix of fire and water). The famous mixed-metaphor: "I smell a rat, I see him brewing in the
air; but I shall yet nip him in the bud."
- **14a: Do not mix metaphor with literal statement.** "He was the **father** of Chemistry,
and **brother** to the Earl of Cork" (metaphor then literal kinship).
- **14b: Do not use poetic metaphor for a prosaic subject.** You may say "a poet **soars**," but
not "Consols **soared** to 94½." Commonplace subjects take commonplace metaphor: "Consols
**mounted/jumped** to 94½."

## Applying this skill
When editing a text against WORDS: go rule by rule 1→14b, mark each violation, and rewrite
the precise word or phrase. When in doubt about force vs. exactness, prefer exactness
(Rule 1) — and never sacrifice clarity for a metaphor you can't sustain.
