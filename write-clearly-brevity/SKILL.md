# Write Clearly — BREVITY (Rules 46–56)

Part III. Brevity is good, but **Rule 56 governs: clearness is the first consideration.**
Several brevity rules clash with clarity rules — when they do, clarity wins (Horace: "While
I take pains to be brief, I fall into obscurity").

## 46. Metaphor is briefer than literal statement
"The cares and responsibilities of a sovereign often disturb his sleep" → "**Uneasy lies the
head that wears a crown**" (effect of care assimilated to a heavy crown). See Rule 13.

## 47. General terms are briefer, though less forcible, than particular terms
"He **devours literature, no matter of what kind**" is shorter than "Novels or sermons, poems
or histories, no matter what, he devours them all." (Trade-off: specific terms from Rule 11 are more forcible but
longer. Brevity and force pull opposite ways; choose by priority.)

## 47a. A phrase may be expressed by a word
"can never be forgotten" → **indelible**; "of such a nature that it cannot be understood" →
**unintelligible**. Watch the filler phrase "of such a nature that."

## 48. Participles as brief equivalents of conjunction+verb phrases
"**Hearing** (when he heard) this, he advanced." "**This done**, he retired." Can be ambiguous
(see Rule 7) — use only when no ambiguity.

## 49. Participles/Adjectives as equivalents of relative phrases
"the **never-ceasing** wind," "the **clamouring** ocean," "the **drenching** rain." Don't invent
-ing adjectives from nouns in prose ("the crannying wind" is poetry-only).

## 50. A statement may be briefly implied instead of expressed at length
"Christianity was humanizing, and therefore discouraged…" → "Gladiatorial shows were first
discouraged… by the **humanizing spirit of Christianity.**" A name/epithet can carry an
implied statement ("the hardy mountaineers" = the Swiss, because they were hardy
mountaineers; "the conqueror of Austerlitz" vs "the prisoner of St. Helena"). **Caution:**
don't use periphrases for the same person unless each is apt in its context (avoid "the
Merry Monarch" when simply "he" is meant).

## 51. Conjunctions may be omitted
Omission gives forcible abruptness: "You say this: I deny it." In short sentences (Macaulay)
omission is fine. Where a contrast is intended, keep "but"; using "and" instead gives an
epigrammatic incongruity ("He always talks truthfully and prosily").

## 51a. The Imperative may be used for "if"
"**Strip** Virtue of the awful authority she derives from the general reverence of mankind,
and you rob her of half her majesty" (= if you strip…).

## 52. Apposition may convert two sentences into one
"We called at the house of a person…, **a musician, and, what is more, a good friend** to all
young students of music" (instead of "He was a musician…").

## 53. Condensation by not repeating a common Subject/Object
(1) Common subject: "he resided…, and, after he had won the esteem of all, died." (2) Common
object: "He came **to**, and was induced to reside **in**, this city." Caution: such
condensation can cause obscurity, and pausing on light words (to, in) is harsh.

## 54. Tautology — avoid unnecessary repetition
Two kinds of fault: (a) repeating the **same word** needlessly ("this is a painful
**circumstance**… a **circumstance**… the **circumstance**"); (b) **worse** — repeating the
**same meaning** in different words ("a burning thirst for conquests… an **ardent passion**";
"universal opinion of all men"; "so infallible that it is never deceived"). The remedy is not
to vary the words but to **restructure** so nothing is repeated: "This is a painful
circumstance, a circumstance that causes me, and will cause him, deep regret."

## 55. Parenthesis may aid brevity
"We are all (**and who would not be?**) offended at the treatment we have received" — shorter
than a separate sentence. Caution: a parenthesis must not obscure a long sentence.

## 56. Caution: let clearness be the first consideration
Do not aim chiefly at being brief or forcible; aim at being **perfectly clear**. Forcible
style springs from (1) **vividness** and (2) **exactness** of thought, plus the corresponding
exactness in words.
- **Vividness:** when describing, *see* it and describe what you see. Was the killed man
  *executed, cut down, run through the body, butchered, shot, or hanged*? Was the city *stormed,
  surprised, surrendered, starved out, or demolished*? Was the army *repelled, defeated,
  routed, crushed, or annihilated*?
- **Exactness:** know the precise meaning and difference of the words you use.

## Applying this skill
To tighten a draft: go 46→56. Compress only where clarity is untouched; when brevity and
clarity clash, cut the brevity, not the clarity. Delete tautology (both word and meaning
repetition). Prefer the single exact word. End by asking Abbott's vividness test: is the
verb the exact, vivid verb for what actually happened?
