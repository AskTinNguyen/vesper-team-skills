# Cinematic Master Shots

## Overview

Turn any beat into a specific camera setup with Kenworthy's three-volume vocabulary of framing, lens, and move. Master Shots treats the camera as a story tool: each setup exists to reveal character, sell the action, escalate tension, or move the audience's attention, never to decorate.

## Workflow

**1. Name the scene need.**
Identify the dramatic job of the beat: action credibility, suspense, a power shift, a dialogue turn, an entrance or exit reveal, or an emotion that must be read on the face. Read `references/chapter-map.md` to find which volume and chapter families apply.

**2. Ground the beat in the story.**
Ask what the camera must make the audience feel and learn. Let the character's goal, the turning point, and the scene's emotional temperature drive the setup, not the reverse.

**3. Select the setup family.**
Use `references/setup-selection.md` to match the scene type to concrete setups (long-lens work for fights and chases, circling for arguments, object guides for directing attention). Prefer the cheapest setup that fully delivers the beat.

**4. Specify lens, framing, and move.**
Define each selected setup as size, lens, height, and move. Volume 1 delivers high-impact moves per scene family, Volume 2 specializes in dialogue, and Volume 3 covers advanced lensing, depth staging, expert framing, and complex moves.

**5. Layer setups beat by beat.**
Build the shot list as a sequence of setups, one per story turn, not a pile of cool moves. Vary size and move so each beat escalates; keep the 180-degree line and eyelines consistent so the editor can cut.

**6. Stress-test against schedule.**
Confirm every setup is necessary and shootable. If a plan needs several complex moves where one disciplined master plus coverage would serve, simplify.

## Camera Language

- A long lens narrows the frame and flattens distance, making moves feel faster and fights and chases more immediate.
- A short lens exaggerates space and speed; camera moves read faster and environments loom larger.
- Blended and matched moves keep a moving scene coherent; crossing the axis without re-establishment breaks the cut.
- Height and framing are storytelling: low angles empower, the frame's limits sell impact, and showing it without showing it builds suspense.
