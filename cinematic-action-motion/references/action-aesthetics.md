# Action Aesthetics (Bordwell, Planet Hong Kong Ch 8)

Source: David Bordwell, *Planet Hong Kong*, Chapter 8, "Motion Emotion: The Art of the Action Movie."

## Motion emotion

The payoff of a good action scene is emotional, not just visceral. "What makes Hong Kong action scenes so thrilling" cannot be explained by gore alone; the staging, editing, and rhythm should make the audience feel skill and danger as elation. Bordwell's question is how the films "work us up," and the answer is craft: legible staging, rhythm, and visible risk.

## Long take vs constructive editing

- **Long take:** film extended combat in a single shot with a panning camera. It displays the flow of choreography and proves real skill, which is why Bruce Lee insisted on longer takes and more distant views. It is hardest on location and easiest in a rehearsed studio set.
- **Constructive editing:** build a sense of the whole action from separate shots, with no establishing view. The audience mentally assembles the pieces. It lets ordinary actors look skilled, fakes superhuman feats (flights, palm power), and substitutes doubles. Aficionados often dismiss it as fakery, yet it is a mainstay of Hong Kong action cinema.

Both obey "international norms of popular cinema": simple, legible compositions combined by continuity editing.

## Rhythm and the King Boxer model

Even a journeyman film can be masterful. In *King Boxer* (1972), the climactic air battle is told in seven shots:

- Mirror-image parallel leaps establish a brisk symmetry between fighters.
- A third shot maps out the imminent clash.
- One blow per shot, with reverse shots of the fighters in midair.
- The key punch gets the scene's shortest shot (seven frames), while the defeated fighter tumbles over nearly twice as long (twelve frames). Brevity makes the blow feel powerful by making its effects last.
- After even-handed parallel shots at the scene's start, devoting two shots to the loser's fall breaks the symmetry and reads as defeat; we do not need to see the winner land.

Use this as the model: short shots for force, longer shots for consequence, and a deliberate asymmetry to privilege the hero.

## Controlling motion and speed

- Slow motion was a staple from the late 1960s onward; Hong Kong filmmakers often controlled speed during printing (cheaper, and it allowed blurred contours).
- Slow motion used as fine cutting smooths bumpy handheld moves and reads as "quality," but overuse becomes padding (slow strutting and billowing coats).
- Speeded-up motion and reverse motion power comic and impossible feats, such as a tile caught midair or a dagger already "in flight."
- Conventions like the "Charlie bar" and shooting into the sun conceal wirework.

## Visible danger and stylization

- Hong Kong filmmakers put real risk on screen, from daredevil stunts to shooting actual fire sequences. Jackie Chan's charm rests on harrowing stunts plus rerun outtakes.
- Action is stylized and theatrical, from Peking Opera acrobatics to "Hong Kong physics" (fighters soar, fall in spins, fight on cliffs or fence tips). Western "realism" that dissipates energy through fatigue and awkwardness is one option, not a default.
- Borrowing is a strength: Hong Kong action absorbed samurai swordplay, Italian Westerns, New Hollywood gloss, and American action hits, then reworked them.
