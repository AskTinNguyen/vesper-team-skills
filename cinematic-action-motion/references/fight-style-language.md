# Fighting Style Language (Planet Hong Kong Ch 8)

Source: David Bordwell, *Planet Hong Kong*, Chapter 8. The Chinese martial arts are wushu ("crafts of combat"); the colloquial Cantonese kung-fu means combat based on hitting and kicking.

## Core classification

- **Internal vs external:** internal (passive) arts turn the attacker's force against itself with a low center of breathing, circular movement, and knowledge of vital points (t'ai chi). External arts use strong, penetrating attacks with a high center of breathing and explosive release.
- **Soft vs hard:** soft arts (within internal) redirect; hard arts force meeting force in straight lines, using not only arms and legs but also knees, shoulders, and head. Most kung-fu films display the hard approach.

## Northern Leg, Southern Fist

- **Northern schools (legwork):** legs have longer reach and thicker muscle, delivering devastating kicks; arms defend while the fighter jumps, twists, spins, and kicks. Northern styles fed the acrobatics of Peking Opera and the famous flying kick.
- **Southern schools (fist work):** rely more on the upper body with sturdy stances and intricate arm and hand techniques; kicks tend to be low. Famous examples include Hung Gar (Wong Fei-hung) and Wing Chun (Bruce Lee's early style).
- The split is not absolute; systems merge (e.g., Praying Mantis combines balanced footwork with grabbing and clawing). On film, a fighter may mix northern and southern techniques freely.

## Styling for the camera

- What "looks good" is judged from theatrical spectacle as much as combat reality. Peking Opera correlated each role with set movements broken into precise gestures; lyrics were treated as time-movement units.
- Choreographers mix styles depending on camera angle and distance. As Yuen Wah notes, "In films you can't play just one style... it often depends on the camera angle or your distance from the camera as to what looks good."
- Many leading Hong Kong action filmmakers trained in Peking Opera academies (Jackie Chan, Sammo Hung, Yuen Biao, Yuen Kwai, Yuen Wah), which is why acrobatics and stage disciplines shape screen fights.

## Story uses

- A hero can discover a technique by observing an animal's attack (e.g., Jackie Chan in *Snake in the Eagle's Shadow*), turning the fight into discovery.
- Choose the fighting style to express the character: a peace-loving Confucian master uses kung-fu as a last resort; a volatile hero fights with explosive hard-style energy; a comic hero blends acrobatics and improvisation.

## Genre continuity

The action genres are continuous: 1980s gunplay is indebted to older martial-arts movies, and many crime-movie stars and directors began in kung-fu or swordplay films. Style evolves by borrowing and reworking, from anti-real wirework to handheld urban thriller shooting.
