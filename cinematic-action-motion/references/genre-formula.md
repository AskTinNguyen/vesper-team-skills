# Genre, Formula, and Norm (Planet Hong Kong Ch 6)

Source: David Bordwell, *Planet Hong Kong*, Chapter 6, "Formula, Form, and Norm."

## Formula, form, norm

Popular cinema lives on the interplay of formula and form. Genres provide shared expectations (the formula), while each film seeks innovation inside that framework of familiarity. A genre gains prominence and a host of possibilities opens up; competitors respond by varying the known elements.

## Cycles and their short shelf life

Because Hong Kong movies were conceived and shot quickly, cycles had brief lives and spawned rapid spinoffs:

- The gambling cycle after *God of Gamblers* (1989) produced dozens of titles in 1990-1992.
- The revived historical kung-fu saga (After *Once upon a Time in China*, 1991) flooded 1993 with Jet Li vehicles and parodies.
- Packaging could imply a cycle: a horror specialist tagged films with specific dates to group them.

Use this to reason about a scene's genre rhythm: match the audience's expectation, then vary one element to feel fresh without breaking trust.

## Multigenre and export appeal

- Producers built "multigenre" movies to satisfy broad local and overseas audiences; *Aces Go Places* (1982) was an influential action-comedy model.
- Virtually no Hong Kong movie stayed a pure genre piece; at minimum there were dashes of comedy, and even an intimate drama might throw in a fight or chase for export.
- When an action scene has to travel, blend in a comic or plot beat rather than run one tone to its end.

## Intertextuality and parody

- Genre pictures talk to one another; iconography, references, and shared motifs accumulate and can rewrite a genre tradition (e.g., gangster brands, or *Mongkok Story* re-reading *A Better Tomorrow*).
- Parody is a constant source of renewal, from Jackie Chan's *Half a Loaf of Kung-Fu* (1978) satirizing martial-arts credit sequences to comedies deflating heroic cycles.
- Draw on parody deliberately when an action beat needs humor or needs to critique the very genre it is using.

## Market and stars

- Market conditions and industrial pressure shape cycles: female-cop movies, relationship movies, and Category III exploitation each emerged from specific commercial motives.
- Stars anchor genres and travel to foreign markets. Loyal audiences give popular cinema a durable base; design action around the performer's signature (stunt danger, acrobatic comedy, elegant wushu) because the star is a genre element in itself.

## Applying it to a scene

Name the genre promise you are fulfilling, the one element you are renovating, and the audience you are trying to reach. Then the action can be familiar enough to be legible and fresh enough to feel new, with the right blend of tones for the market you are writing for.
